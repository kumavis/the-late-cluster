# The experiment: ten seconds with no words

## What we try

In every segment drafted from a `made` card, the three standing segments aside, give the thing one stretch of at least ten seconds, unbroken by a spoken or sung word, in which it is shown in use, and put the segment's turn inside that stretch, as something the thing does. No line before the stretch gives the turn away and no line after it describes what was just seen; the writer names that turn, in five words, in the segment's notes. The interview, Top Chicken and the song are drafted as the bible has them, whatever card they draw on.

## Why

**What the bible says.** Joke craft gives every punch to the voice: "The voice says the setup and the punch", while the screen "shows the evidence". Choosing material offers a test, "could ten seconds with no words show what it is?", and applies it on paper, when material is picked. This episode runs that test on air and asks one thing more of the ten seconds: that they land the punch.

**This window can bear it.** Nearly every card on the shortlist is a thing that moves: a pen crossing a page and travelling back to the margin, a chess board that is a strip with one side, instruments built from solids, a game in which you have a coffee. The people who watched them answered in very few words. A replier under the plotter wrote of "the suspense while it tracks back to the left side". One member's whole reply to the coffee simulator was "very peaceful thank you". The polyhedra video was posted with no text at all.

**The engine favours it.** The voice loses about one word in five and the pictures are crisp (voices; joke craft). A punch with no words in it is the one punch the engine cannot smear. And in a show that speaks about two and a half words a second, ten seconds without one is a change of texture that a viewer will notice and that the rundown wants between segments.

**One variable:** who delivers the punch, the voice or the thing. Everything else holds.

- The stretch shows the thing in use: what it does, not a card, a chart or a caption about it. Draw only what its card establishes, label the drawing as the studio's sketch where the card says the real one has not been seen, and keep the maker's credit and address on screen (R9).
- Nothing in it to read beyond a glance (television).
- House characters may watch, and react with their heads. They do not speak. The audience makes no sound until the stretch is over; a reaction, if the turn earns one, comes after.
- Sound without words is free: a beat track, a hum, a studio one-shot, a creature call, or nothing.
- Never place the stretch between a real person's line and the setup that recreates its occasion (R4).
- It costs about twenty-five spoken words. Pay in words, not in running time. Ten to fifteen seconds is enough, and the picture keeps changing all the way through: this is a demonstration, not a pause.

## Measure

Checked from the built episode by someone who did not propose, write or stage the segment.

**Was it run?** Take each segment in the rundown that was drafted from a `made` card, the three standing segments aside. In its `cues.json` there is an interval of at least 10.0 seconds containing no `lines` entry of kind `say`, `sing` or `shout` and no crowd sound in `sfx` (`laugh`, `biglaugh`, `chuckle`, `groan`, `ooh`, `aww`, `woo`, `applause`, `gasp`, `hey`, `murmur`, `silence-cough`). Frames shot at the start, the middle and the end of that interval show the thing in use, differ from one another, carry the maker's credit, and hold no text beyond a glance. *Run:* every such segment passes. *Partly run:* some do. *Not run:* none does, or the rundown has no such segment.

**Did it work?** A cold viewer, who has read no script, card or notes for the segment, is given only the stretch (its frames in order, one a second, and its sound) and writes one sentence: what the thing is, and what happened. It worked in that segment if the sentence names the turn the writer's five words named. The experiment *worked* if that is so for more than half of the stretches and *did not work* if it is so for none; anything between is *can't tell*. All three are results.
