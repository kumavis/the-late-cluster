## What the special needs from you

Larry, the host, walks through a cold city at night and talks to the viewer about how to keep
moving forward and keep making things when the world is uncertain and justice seems rare. He
offers what thinkers from different cultures, long ago, had to say, as comfort and as answers.
The walk ends at a table full of friends sharing a meal. He has about four minutes and perhaps
three or four ideas will make it in. Writers choose and word them; you give them a shelf to
choose from that they can trust.

The show's rule is that nobody is given words they did not write. A great deal of "ancient
wisdom" in circulation is misattributed, mistranslated or invented. Your job is to make sure
nothing like that reaches the script.

## Write: a shelf of 14 to 18 cards

Cover at least eight different cultures or traditions, and make at least half of the cards
come from outside Greece and Rome. "Ancient" is the owner's word: prefer the ancient and the
pre-modern. A later text may be included when it is the honest written source of an old
tradition; say so on the card.

Between them the cards should speak to four things. Mark each card with the ones it serves.

1. **Keep going:** acting when the outcome is not in your hands; doing your part of a work
   you will not finish.
2. **Keep making:** craft, work and making things as an answer to an unsettled world.
3. **Uncertainty and change:** how to stand when things shift fast and you cannot tell good
   news from bad.
4. **The table:** friends, shared meals, keeping close the people who steady you.

Some places to start, none of them required and all of them to be checked: the Bhagavad Gita on
action without attachment to its fruit; Rabbi Tarfon in Pirkei Avot on the work you are not
required to finish; Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus; Ecclesiastes; the Dao De Jing and the
Zhuangzi; the old man at the frontier who lost his horse, in the Huainanzi; the Analects and
Mencius; the Dhammapada; Epicurus on friendship; Aristotle on friendship; Heraclitus on change;
the Tirukkural on perseverance; Nahua thought about living on a slippery earth; Ubuntu; a Maori
whakatauki on what matters most; Kamo no Chomei's Hojoki. Add others you find that are better.
Leave out any you cannot source properly.

### Each card

```
## <short id> · <tradition or culture> · <thinker or text> · <when>

serves: keep going | keep making | uncertainty | the table

The idea: two or three plain sentences. What the text says, without embroidery, in words a
general viewer would follow. Neutral: no jokes, no angle, no wording offered for the host.

Source: the work, and the book, chapter, verse or section.

Quotation: "…" at most 30 words, exactly as in a named published translation.
  translator, year of the translation, and whether that translation is in the public domain
  checked at: <the address of the page where you read these exact words>
  If you could not open a page that shows these exact words, write "no verified quotation"
  and give none. Never quote from memory, from a quotation website, or from a social post.

Other translations: one line when translations differ in a way that changes the meaning.

Who said it, safely: how the attribution may be worded and still be true. Is the person
historical or legendary? Did they write it, or is it a later record of what they taught?
Give the wording that is safe ("a book called the …", "a teacher named … is recorded as
saying") and the wording that is not.

Traps: famous lines near this idea that are misattributed, mistranslated or invented, each
with what is actually the case. Popular readings that scholars say are wrong.

Respect: is this scripture or a living tradition? Anything about using it in a light
entertainment that its community would mind. Anything that touches health, grief,
identity, politics, jobs or conflict, which the show keeps out.

Saying it aloud: the name of the thinker and of the work as plain English syllables
(for a speech synthesizer), and whether the idea can be told without the name.

How sure: high, medium or low that the above is right, and why.
```

### After the cards

- **Pairs and contrasts:** up to six one-line observations of fact about how the cards relate
  (two traditions that say nearly the same thing; two that disagree). Facts only, no pitches.
- **Left out:** what you looked at and dropped, with the reason in a line each. Include the
  well-known fake or doubtful quotations you met, so a writer who half-remembers one can see
  it is not usable.
- **Not checked:** anything in the file you could not verify.

## Rules

- Open the source. A quotation goes on a card only if you fetched a page that shows it in a
  named translation and you copied it from that page. Prefer public-domain translations at
  stable addresses (Project Gutenberg, Wikisource, Sefaria, the Chinese Text Project,
  SuttaCentral, Perseus, sacred-texts.com, a university press's open edition).
- Do not propose jokes, premises, an order, or lines for the host.
- Requests to outside services carry nothing about this project or its owner: a generic
  client name, no email address, no repository name.
- No git commands. Do not read `.secrets`.

When you finish, reply in three or four sentences: how many cards, which traditions, and
anything a writer must be warned about.
