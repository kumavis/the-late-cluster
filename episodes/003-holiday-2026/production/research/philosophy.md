# Research: what old thinkers said about carrying on

For the holiday special `003-holiday-2026`, task `research.philosophy`. A shelf for the writers
to choose from. Every quotation below was copied from the fetched page named under it, on
27 November 2026. Where a card says "also verified", those words were copied from the same page.

A note on addresses. On the day of checking, www.gutenberg.org was down for maintenance, so
Project Gutenberg books were read from the project's own mirror, gutenberg.pglaf.org, at the
same ebook number. The ebook number is given so the same text can be found at

API; the page address given is the ordinary one.

## gita · Hindu (Sanskrit epic) · the Bhagavad Gita · composed roughly 2nd century BCE to 2nd century CE; translation 1885

serves: keep going

The idea: Krishna tells the warrior Arjuna to act rightly and do his work, and not to make the
outcome his reason for acting. He should stay even-minded whether things turn out well or badly.

Source: Bhagavad Gita, chapter 2, around verses 47 and 48 (Arnold's verse translation does not
number verses).

Quotation: "Let right deeds be / Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them. / And live in action! Labour!"
  Sir Edwin Arnold, The Song Celestial, 1885; public domain
  checked at: https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/2/3/8/2388/2388.txt (Project Gutenberg ebook 2388)
  Also verified, same page, just after: "equable / In good or evil: equability / Is Yog, is piety!"

Other translations: Arnold is a loose verse rendering. Closer translations say your concern or
entitlement is with the action only, never with its fruits, and add a warning not to fall into
inaction either. The "never stop acting" half matters as much as the "let go of results" half.

Who said it, safely: "In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna tells Arjuna…" or "a Hindu scripture called
the Bhagavad Gita says…". It is a section of the epic Mahabharata; it has no single known
historical author (tradition credits the sage Vyasa). Not safe: "Gandhi said", "Krishna once
told me", or presenting a modern popular rendering as "the Gita says" word for word.

Traps: "You have a right to your work, but not to its fruits" circulates in many wordings; the
idea is right, but the word "right" is a modern translator's choice and is not in Arnold. The
line "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds" (Oppenheimer's paraphrase of chapter 11) is
nearby in people's memory and belongs to war; keep it out.

Respect: scripture for hundreds of millions of living Hindus. The setting is a battlefield and
the advice is given to a man about to fight; the special should not dwell on the setting
(conflict is out of bounds). Treat Krishna as a deity, not a character.

Saying it aloud: BUH-guh-vud GEE-tah; KRISH-nuh; AR-joo-nuh. The idea can be told without the
name ("an old Indian scripture").

How sure: high. Verse location and wording checked; the reading is the standard one.

## avot · Jewish (rabbinic) · Rabbi Tarfon in Pirkei Avot · Tarfon lived c. 1st–2nd century CE; the Mishnah was compiled c. 200 CE; translation 1913

serves: keep going | keep making

The idea: Rabbi Tarfon says the day is short and the work is great. You are not obliged to finish
the work yourself, but you are not free to stop doing your part either.

Source: Mishnah, tractate Avot (Pirkei Avot, "Sayings of the Fathers"), chapter 2. Numbered
2:15–16 in most editions; Gorfinkle numbers them 2:20–21.

Quotation: "It is not thy duty to complete the work, but neither art thou free to desist from it"
  Joseph I. Gorfinkle, The Sayings of the Jewish Fathers, first published 1913 (the e-text is
  the second edition, undated on the page); public domain in the US
  checked at: https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/8/5/4/8548/8548.txt (Project Gutenberg ebook 8548)
  Also verified, same page: "The day is short, the task is great, the laborers are sluggish, the
  reward is much, and the Master of the house is urgent." (The e-text misspells the name
  "Tafron"; it is Tarfon.)

Other translations: Some render it "It is not your duty to finish the work"; others "the work is
not yours to complete". In the original the "work" is the study and practice of Torah, with a
reward from God; the general reading about any long task is a widening.

Who said it, safely: "a rabbi named Tarfon, nearly two thousand years ago, is recorded as saying…"
He is a historical figure; the words are a record of his teaching compiled after his death. Not
safe: "the Talmud says" (Avot is in the Mishnah), "a Jewish proverb", or any story about Tarfon's
life.

Traps: "Do not be daunted by the enormity of the world's grief. Do justly, now. Love mercy, now.
Walk humbly, now. You are not obligated to complete the work, but neither are you free to
abandon it." circulates online as "the Talmud". It is a modern free paraphrase (credited to
Rabbi Rami Shapiro, 1990s) that adds lines from Micah. Only the last sentence is Tarfon. Also it
names grief and justice, which the special keeps out.

Respect: Avot is read in synagogues on summer Sabbaths and is part of the living Jewish liturgy.
Fine to quote with reverence; do not joke about the rabbi.

Saying it aloud: RAH-bye TAR-fon; PEER-kay ah-VOTE. Can be told without the names ("an old
rabbi").

How sure: high for wording and source; medium for the exact year of the second edition.

## eccl · Hebrew Bible · Ecclesiastes (Qohelet) · probably 5th–3rd century BCE; translation 1611

serves: keep going | uncertainty | the table

The idea: You cannot tell which of your efforts will come to anything, so keep sowing, morning
and evening. Elsewhere the same book says two people are better than one, because one can lift
the other up, and tells the reader to eat bread and drink wine with a glad heart.

Source: Ecclesiastes 11:6 (sowing); 11:4 (watching the wind); 4:9–10 (two better than one); 9:7
(eat thy bread with joy).

Quotation: "In the morning sow thy seed, and in the evening withhold not thine hand: for thou knowest not whether shall prosper, either this or that"
  King James Version, 1611; public domain (in the UK the KJV is under Crown letters patent, which
  does not affect a short quotation)
  checked at: https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/1/10/10-0.txt (Project Gutenberg ebook 10)
  Also verified, same page: "He that observeth the wind shall not sow; and he that regardeth the
  clouds shall not reap." (11:4) / "Two are better than one" and "if they fall, the one will lift
  up his fellow" (4:9–10) / "Go thy way, eat thy bread with joy, and drink thy wine with a merry
  heart" (9:7)

Other translations: Modern translations say much the same; 11:1 ("cast thy bread upon the
waters") is read by some as advice to trade by sea and by others as advice to be generous; its
meaning is disputed.

Who said it, safely: "the book of Ecclesiastes says…" or "the Preacher in Ecclesiastes". The
book presents itself as the words of "the Preacher, son of David, king in Jerusalem"; scholars
do not think Solomon wrote it. Not safe: "King Solomon said".

Traps: "Eat, drink and be merry, for tomorrow we die" is not in Ecclesiastes; it blends 8:15
with Isaiah 22:13. 9:7 sits next to verses about death and the grave (9:10); quote only the meal.
4:11, right after "two are better than one", is about two lying together for warmth; it reads
as a joke today and is better left out.

Respect: scripture for Jews and Christians. Short quotation with its book named is ordinary
respectful use.

Saying it aloud: ee-klee-zee-AS-teez. Can be told as "an old book of the Bible".

How sure: high.

## ddj · Chinese (Daoist) · the Dao De Jing, credited to Laozi · probably 4th–3rd century BCE; translation 1891

serves: keep going | uncertainty

The idea: Great things grow from very small beginnings, so begin with the step in front of you.
Elsewhere the book says good fortune and bad fortune lie inside each other, and nobody knows how
either will end.

Source: Dao De Jing, chapter 64 (the thousand li); chapter 58 (misery and happiness).

Quotation: "the journey of a thousand li commenced with a single step."
  James Legge, The Tao Teh King, Sacred Books of the East vol. 39, 1891; public domain
  checked at: https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/2/1/216/216.txt (Project Gutenberg ebook 216)
  Also verified, same page, the full sentence: "The tree which fills the arms grew from the
  tiniest sprout; the tower of nine storeys rose from a (small) heap of earth; the journey of a
  thousand li commenced with a single step." And chapter 58: "Misery!--happiness is to be found
  by its side! Happiness!--misery lurks beneath it! Who knows what either will come to in the
  end?"

Other translations: The Chinese says the journey "begins beneath the feet" (始於足下). "A single
step" is the translators' smoothing; the meaning holds. A "li" is a Chinese distance, about a
third of a mile; most popular versions say "a thousand miles".

Who said it, safely: "an old Chinese book called the Dao De Jing" or "the book traditionally
credited to Laozi". Laozi may be legendary; the book is likely a compilation. Not safe: "Laozi
wrote", "Confucius said" (often confused), or any story of Laozi riding off on an ox as fact.

Traps: Not in the Dao De Jing, though widely credited to Laozi: "Nature does not hurry, yet
everything is accomplished"; "When I let go of what I am, I become what I might be"; "New
beginnings are often disguised as painful endings". The thousand-li line is real; its "miles"
wording is not Legge's.

Respect: a scripture of religious Daoism, practised today, and a philosophical classic. Light
use is common and generally fine.

Saying it aloud: DOW duh JING (the "Dao" rhymes with "how"); LAOW-dzuh. The thousand-li line can
be told without any name.

How sure: high.

## zhuangzi · Chinese (Daoist) · the Zhuangzi, the cook who carved the ox · c. 4th–3rd century BCE; translation 1889

serves: keep making | uncertainty

The idea: A prince praises his cook's skill at carving an ox. The cook says that after years of
practice he follows the natural joints, so his blade has stayed sharp for nineteen years, and
when he meets a hard place he slows down, looks closely and moves gently.

Source: Zhuangzi, chapter 3 ("Nourishing the Lord of Life").

Quotation: "when I come upon a hard part where the blade meets with a difficulty, I am all caution. I fix my eye on it. I stay my hand, and gently apply my blade"
  Herbert A. Giles, Chuang Tzŭ: Mystic, Moralist, and Social Reformer, 1889; public domain
  checked at: https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/5/9/7/0/59709/59709-0.txt (Project Gutenberg ebook 59709)
  Also verified, same page: "I have had this chopper nineteen years, and although I have cut up
  many thousand bullocks, its edge is as if fresh from the whetstone."

Other translations: Legge and later translators call the cook "Ding" (Cook Ding), which may be a
name or a word for "cook". The prince's closing line is that he has learned how to nourish life;
Giles adds his own gloss ("the soul").

Who said it, safely: "a story in the Zhuangzi" or "the Chinese philosopher Zhuangzi tells a
story". Zhuangzi (Zhuang Zhou) was a historical person, but the book was assembled over time and
only the first seven chapters are usually credited to him; chapter 3 is one of those. The cook
is a figure in a parable, not a recorded person.

Traps: The butterfly dream is also real (chapter 2), but is often quoted in invented modern
wordings. "Flow with whatever may happen and let your mind be free" circulates as Zhuangzi; it is
a loose modern paraphrase of a line in chapter 4, not a translation.

Respect: Daoist classic; parables are meant to delight. The story is about butchering, described
in detail; for a meal scene keep it to the knife and the patience, not the carcass.

Saying it aloud: JWAHNG-dzuh. Can be told as "an old Chinese story about a cook".

How sure: high.

## horse · Chinese (Han dynasty) · the Huainanzi, the old man at the frontier who lost his horse · compiled c. 139 BCE

serves: uncertainty

The idea: A man near the frontier loses his horse and his father asks how they know it is not good
luck; the horse returns with fine horses, and he asks how they know it is not bad luck. The son
breaks his leg riding, and later, when the young men are called to war, the son is spared. The
book's lesson is that fortune and misfortune turn into each other and cannot be predicted.

Source: Huainanzi, chapter 18, "Ren jian xun" (人間訓, "Among Men").

Quotation: no verified quotation. The Chinese text was read at
  https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/淮南子/人間訓 ; it ends "故福之為禍，禍之為福，化不可極，深不可測也".
  The complete English translation (Major and others, Columbia University Press, 2010) is in
  copyright and was not opened; no public-domain English translation of chapter 18 was found.

Other translations: Retellings differ on who speaks: in the Chinese it is the father (其父) of a
man "skilled in arts" (善術者, often read as divination) who says each line, not "a farmer".

Who said it, safely: "an old Chinese book, the Huainanzi, tells a story" or "there is an old
Chinese story". The book was compiled at the court of Liu An, King of Huainan; the men in the
story are not historical. The Chinese idiom 塞翁失馬 ("the old man at the frontier loses his
horse") came later. Not safe: "a Zen story", "a Taoist farmer", or any "Maybe."-style dialogue
put in quotation marks.

Traps: The popular version in which a farmer answers every neighbour with "Maybe" (often from
Alan Watts) is a modern retelling; there is no "maybe" in the Huainanzi. It is sometimes called
a Zen koan; it is not one.

Respect: no living-scripture concern. But the story turns on a broken leg and on a war in which
"nine in ten" of the frontier men die (死者十九); both are out of bounds for the special
(health, conflict). The middle of the story can be told without the ending, but then it is a
telling, not the book's story, and should be said so.

Saying it aloud: HWHY-nahn-dzuh. Easily told without the name.

How sure: high for the Chinese text and its source; there is no checked English wording.

## analects · Chinese (Confucian) · the Analects of Confucius · compiled c. 5th–3rd century BCE; translation 1861

serves: keep going | uncertainty | the table

The idea: A gatekeeper describes Confucius as the man who knows his times will not let his work
succeed and keeps doing it anyway. Elsewhere Confucius compares learning to raising a mound: if
he stops one basket short, the stopping is his own; if he adds one basket, that going forward is
his own. The book opens by calling it a delight when friends come from far away.

Source: Analects, book 14, chapter 41 in Legge (14.38 in some numberings); book 9, chapter 18
(9.19 elsewhere); book 1, chapter 1; book 9, chapter 16 (the stream).

Quotation: "who knows the impracticable nature of the times and yet will be doing in them."
  James Legge, The Chinese Classics vol. 1, first published 1861; public domain
  checked at: https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/4/0/9/4094/4094-0.txt (Project Gutenberg ebook 4094)
  Also verified, same page: "'Is it not delightful to have friends coming from distant
  quarters?'" (1.1) / "If there want but one basket of earth to complete the work, and I stop,
  the stopping is my own work." (9.18) / "The Master standing by a stream, said, 'It passes on
  just like this, not ceasing day or night!'" (9.16)

Other translations: The gatekeeper line is often given as "the one who knows it can't be done
and still does it" (知其不可而為之); Legge's "impracticable nature of the times" is stiffer but
faithful. In 1.1, "friends" (朋) can also mean fellow students.

Who said it, safely: "the Analects, a record of what Confucius and his students said" or "Confucius
is recorded as saying". Confucius is historical; he wrote none of it. The gatekeeper line is
spoken by an unnamed gatekeeper about Confucius, not by Confucius. Not safe: "Confucius wrote".

Traps: "It does not matter how slowly you go as long as you do not stop" is everywhere as
Confucius and is not in the Analects. "Choose a job you love, and you will never have to work a
day in your life" is not Confucius. "Our greatest glory is not in never falling" is not
Confucius (it is usually traced to Oliver Goldsmith, 18th century).

Respect: Confucianism is a living ethical and ritual tradition. Fine to quote.

Saying it aloud: kun-FYOO-shus; AN-uh-lekts. The mound and the friends can be told without
the name.

How sure: high.

## dhammapada · Buddhist (Pali canon) · the Dhammapada · teachings attributed to the Buddha (5th century BCE), compiled perhaps 3rd century BCE; translation 1881

serves: uncertainty | keep making

The idea: A wise person is like a solid rock in the wind and is not shaken by blame or by praise.
Another verse says a water-pot fills drop by drop, and so a person fills with good a little at a
time.

Source: Dhammapada, verse 81 (chapter 6, "The Wise Man"); verse 122 (chapter 9, "Evil").

Quotation: "As a solid rock is not shaken by the wind, wise people falter not amidst blame and praise."
  F. Max Müller, The Dhammapada, Sacred Books of the East vol. 10, 1881; public domain
  checked at: https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/2/0/1/2017/2017-0.txt (Project Gutenberg ebook 2017)
  Also verified, same page, verse 122: "Even by the falling of water-drops a water-pot is filled;
  the wise man becomes full of good, even if he gather it little by little."

Other translations: Modern translations (for example Sujato's, free on SuttaCentral) agree in
sense. Verse 122 is the twin of verse 121, which uses the same image for a fool filling with
evil; quote 122, not the pair loosely.

Who said it, safely: "a collection of the Buddha's sayings called the Dhammapada" or "the
Dhammapada, an early Buddhist book of verses". The Buddha is historical; the verses were passed
down orally and written later. Not safe: "the Buddha wrote".

Traps: The Buddha is the most misquoted figure in the field. Not his words: "Holding on to anger
is like grasping a hot coal" (a later commentator's image, much reworded); "Thousands of candles
can be lit from a single candle"; "Peace comes from within. Do not seek it without"; "Three
things cannot be long hidden". None of these should be used as "the Buddha said".

Respect: scripture for living Theravada Buddhists. Do not put the Buddha's image or name in a
gag.

Saying it aloud: DUM-uh-PAH-duh; the BOO-duh. Can be told without the name.

How sure: high.

## kural-effort · Tamil · the Tirukkural, chapter on effort · usually dated between c. 300 BCE and 500 CE (disputed); translation 1886

serves: keep going | keep making

The idea: Do not say a thing is too hard; effort itself gives the strength to do it. Do not give
up partway through a work, because the world leaves those who leave their work unfinished. Those
who keep at it without fear see even fate put behind them.

Source: Tirukkural, chapter 62, "Manly Effort" (Āḷviṉaiyuṭaimai), couplets 611, 612, 619, 620.

Quotation: "In action be thou, ‘ware of act’s defeat; / The world leaves those who work leave incomplete!"
  G. U. Pope, The Sacred Kurral of Tiruvalluva-Nayanar, 1886 (verse); public domain
  checked at: https://archive.org/download/tirukkural-tiruvalluvar/Tirukkural_George%20Uglow%20Pope_djvu.txt (couplet 612; the e-text heads the chapter "2.1.24 Manly Effort")
  Also verified, same page, 611: "Say not, “Tis hard’, in weak, desponding hour, / For strenuous
  effort gives prevailing power." / 620: "Who strive with undismayed, unfaltering mind, / At
  length shall leave opposing fate behind." / the plain prose under 612: "the world will abandon
  those who abandon their unfinished work."

Other translations: The verse is Pope's; the prose line under each couplet in this online
edition is the prose translation of Drew and Lazarus, which the edition credits jointly with
Pope and Ellis. The prose is plainer and easier to say.

Who said it, safely: "a Tamil book of couplets called the Tirukkural", "the Tirukkural,
credited to the poet Tiruvalluvar". Almost nothing is known of Tiruvalluvar's life; his date is
disputed by centuries. Not safe: any detail of his life, or a single firm date.

Traps: The chapter title is Pope's ("Manly"); the Tamil is about persistent effort and is not
about men. Couplet 614 in this chapter compares a man who does not work to "a hermaphrodite" with
a sword; keep it out. The OCR in this e-text garbles couplets 614–618; do not quote from those.

Respect: the Kural is deeply honoured in Tamil Nadu and among Tamils worldwide, and several
religious communities claim Tiruvalluvar as their own; that claim is a live public dispute.
Call him a poet and do not assign him a religion. Do not joke about the book.

Saying it aloud: tih-roo-KOO-rul; tih-roo-VULL-oo-var. The idea can be told as "an old Tamil
poem".

How sure: high for the verse wording and couplet numbers; medium for which translator wrote the
prose line.

## kural-guests · Tamil · the Tirukkural, chapter on cherishing guests · as above; translation 1886

serves: the table

The idea: Even if you had the food of the gods on your table, eating it alone while guests wait
outside would be wrong. The point of keeping a home is to welcome people to it. A cold face at
the door wilts a guest the way smelling wilts a delicate flower.

Source: Tirukkural, chapter 9, "Cherishing Guests" (Virundōmbal), couplets 81, 82, 90.

Quotation: "Though food of immortality should crown the board, / Feasting alone, the guests without unfed, is thing abhorred."
  G. U. Pope, 1886 (verse); public domain
  checked at: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Tirukural/Chapter_9 (also on the archive.org e-text above, couplet 82)
  Also verified, same page, couplet 81: "All household cares and course of daily life have this
  in view. / Guests to receive with courtesy, and kindly acts to do."

Other translations: The prose under 82 reads "It is not fit that one should wish his guests to be
outside (his house) even though he were eating the food of immortality." Couplet 90's flower is
the anicham, which Tamil poetry says wilts when smelled.

Who said it, safely: as the card above.

Traps: Couplet 84 names the goddess Lakshmi; keep religious figures out of a light scene.

Respect: as above.

Saying it aloud: as above; "anicham" is AH-nee-chum.

How sure: high.

## hojoki · Japanese (Buddhist recluse) · Kamo no Chōmei, Hōjōki ("An Account of My Hut") · written 1212; translation 1899

serves: uncertainty

The idea: The book opens by saying a river flows on and never stops, but its water is never the
same, and the foam on it forms and vanishes. People and their houses in a great city are like
that: some are torn down one year and rebuilt the next.

Source: Hōjōki, opening section.

Quotation: "The current of a running stream flows on unceasingly, but the water is not the same"
  W. G. Aston, A History of Japanese Literature, 1899 (Aston translates long passages); public domain
  checked at: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_History_of_Japanese_Literature/Book_4/Chapter_3
  Also verified, same page: "Some were destroyed last year to be rebuilt this year; others, which
  were great houses, have been ruined, and replaced by smaller ones."

Other translations: Later translators (Sadler 1928, Keene 1955, McKinney 2013) give the same
image; Keene's is on Wikisource too, but its copyright status was not checked, so it is not
quoted here.

Who said it, safely: "a Japanese poet, Kamo no Chōmei, who wrote a short book about living in a
tiny hut, more than eight hundred years ago". He is historical and wrote it himself. Not safe:
calling him a Zen master (he was a Pure Land Buddhist lay monk and poet).

Traps: The opening is often blended with Heraclitus's river into one "ancient saying"; they are
two different texts from opposite ends of the world. The book's moral is withdrawal: Chōmei
leaves the city and people to live alone. It does not support the idea of the table.

Respect: no living-scripture concern. But almost the whole of the Hōjōki after the opening
describes fire, whirlwind, famine, plague and earthquake in the capital, with the dead in the
streets. Use the opening only; the rest is out of bounds (health, grief).

Saying it aloud: HO-jo-kee; KAH-mo no CHO-may. Can be told as "a Japanese poet, long ago".

How sure: high.

## havamal · Norse (Old Icelandic) · the Hávamál ("Sayings of the High One") · poems probably older than the 13th-century manuscript (Codex Regius, c. 1270); translation 1923

serves: the table | keep going

The idea: The speaker remembers being young and walking alone, not knowing the road, and feeling
rich the moment he found a companion. The road to a friend is wide and straight even if the friend
lives far off, and you should go and see a trusted friend often, because grass grows over a road
nobody walks.

Source: Hávamál, stanzas 47, 34, 44 and 119 in Bellows' numbering.

Quotation: "Young was I once, and wandered alone, / And nought of the road I knew; / Rich did I feel when a comrade I found, / For man is man's delight."
  Henry Adams Bellows, The Poetic Edda, 1923; public domain in the US
  checked at: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Poetic_Edda_(tr._Bellows)/Hovamol (stanza 47; the page sets a wide space mid-line, shown here as one space)
  Also verified, same page, stanza 34: "But wide and straight is the way to a friend, / Though
  far away he fare." / stanza 119: "If a friend thou hast whom thou fully wilt trust, / Then fare
  to find him oft; / For brambles grow and waving grass / On the rarely trodden road."

Other translations: "Man is man's delight" (maðr er manns gaman) is literal; modern versions say
"a person is a person's joy". Stanza 45 next door advises meeting a false friend with
falsehood; do not quote the friendship stanzas as if the poem were all kindness.

Who said it, safely: "an old Norse poem called the Hávamál". The poem is anonymous; within it the
speaker is the god Odin (the "High One"), who is mythological. Not safe: "the Vikings said",
"Odin said" as if historical, or a named author.

Traps: Stanzas 76–77 ("Cattle die, and kinsmen die") are real and famous, but are about death and
fame; out of bounds here. Modern lists of "Viking wisdom" often add invented lines.

Respect: Norse heathenry is a small living religion and the Hávamál is sacred to some of its
followers. Norse imagery has also been taken up by extremist groups; keep to the text and away
from runes, warriors and symbols.

Saying it aloud: HAH-vah-mahl. Easily told as "an old Norse poem".

How sure: high.

## gilgamesh · Mesopotamian (Babylonian) · the Epic of Gilgamesh, Old Babylonian version · tablet c. 1800 BCE; translation 1920

serves: the table

The idea: The hero Gilgamesh is wandering in search of a way to escape death. A woman who keeps
a tavern by the sea tells him he will not find it, and that he should fill his belly, rejoice day
and night, wear clean clothes, care for his child and his wife.

Source: Old Babylonian "Meissner" tablet (Sippar tablet), corresponding to tablet X of the later
standard version; the speech of the ale-wife Siduri.

Quotation: "Daily make a rejoicing! / Day and night a renewal of jollification!"
  Morris Jastrow Jr. and Albert T. Clay, An Old Babylonian Version of the Gilgamesh Epic, Yale
  Oriental Series, 1920 (Jastrow's translation of the Meissner tablet, in the introduction);
  public domain
  checked at: https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/1/1/0/0/11000/11000-0.txt (Project Gutenberg ebook 11000)
  Also verified, same page: "Thou, O Gish, fill thy belly, / Day and night do thou rejoice"

Other translations: Jastrow calls the woman "Sabitum" (a word for an ale-wife) and the hero
"Gish"; later scholars use the name Siduri and "Gilgamesh". Modern translations (George 1999,
in copyright) read the speech much the same way.

Who said it, safely: "the oldest story we have, the Epic of Gilgamesh, has a tavern-keeper tell
the hero…". The poem is anonymous. Gilgamesh may have been a real early king, but the epic is
legend; Siduri is a character.

Traps: The speech is often quoted as "eat, drink and be merry" and blended with Ecclesiastes; the
two are related in thought, and Jastrow says so on the same page, but they are different texts.

Respect: no living tradition. But the context is grief and the fear of death: Gilgamesh is
mourning his friend Enkidu, and the line just before the quotation is that the gods gave death
to mankind. That is out of bounds; the meal advice can only be used without its context, and a
writer should say so if it is used.

Saying it aloud: GILL-guh-mesh; sih-DOO-ree. Can be told as "the oldest story in the world".

How sure: high for the wording; medium for whether the meal can be used apart from its context.

## marcus · Roman (Stoic) · Marcus Aurelius, Meditations · written c. 170–180 CE; translation 1862

serves: uncertainty | keep going

The idea: Be like a headland that the waves keep breaking against; it stands firm and the water
calms around it. Elsewhere he asks who is afraid of change, when nothing useful, not even a bath or
a meal, can happen without it. And on mornings when he does not want to get up, he reminds himself
he is rising to do the work of a human being.

Source: Meditations, book 4, section 49 (the promontory); book 7, section 18 (change); book 5,
section 1 (rising in the morning).

Quotation: "Be like the promontory against which the waves continually break, but it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it."
  George Long, The Thoughts of the Emperor Marcus Aurelius Antoninus, 1862; public domain
  checked at: https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/1/5/8/7/15877/15877.txt (Project Gutenberg ebook 15877)
  Also verified, same page: "Is any man afraid of change? Why, what can take place without
  change?" (7.18) / "In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be
  present,--I am rising to the work of a human being." (5.1)

Other translations: Casaubon's 1634 version (Gutenberg ebook 2680, also checked) gives the
promontory as "against which though the waves beat continually, yet it both itself stands"; it is
a looser paraphrase. Modern translations (Hays 2002) are in copyright.

Who said it, safely: "the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius, in a private notebook" or "in his
Meditations". He is historical and wrote it himself, to himself, in Greek. Not safe: calling it a
book he published, or putting it on a battlefield.

Traps: Not in the Meditations: "Everything we hear is an opinion, not a fact. Everything we see is
a perspective, not the truth"; "Dwell on the beauty of life"-style lists. "The impediment to
action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way" is a modern translation of 5.20
and is often sharpened further. The bed passage (5.1) goes on about keeping warm in the
bed-clothes; it is genuine and may be tempting for a cold night, but it is book 5, not a joke
by Marcus.

Respect: no living-scripture concern. He wrote much of it on military campaign; leave the war out.

Saying it aloud: MAR-kus aw-REE-lee-us. Can be told as "a Roman emperor".

How sure: high.

## epictetus · Greek (Stoic) · Epictetus, the Enchiridion (Handbook) · teaching c. 100 CE, recorded by his student Arrian; translation 1865

serves: uncertainty | the table

The idea: Some things are in our power, our own opinions, aims and wishes, and many are not,
like our body, property and reputation. Trouble comes from treating what is not ours as if it were.
Behave in life as at a banquet: take a moderate share of what is passed to you, and do not grab
at what has not reached you yet.

Source: Enchiridion, section 1 (what is in our power); section 15 (the banquet).

Quotation: "There are things which are within our power, and there are things which are beyond our power."
  Thomas W. Higginson, The Enchiridion, 1865 (the e-text is a 1955 reprint; the introduction is
  not public domain, the translation is)
  checked at: https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/4/5/1/0/45109/45109-0.txt (Project Gutenberg ebook 45109)
  Also verified, same page, section 15: "Remember that you must behave as at a banquet. Is
  anything brought round to you? Put out your hand and take a moderate share."

Other translations: Older versions say "things in our power" / "not in our power"; modern ones
"up to us" / "not up to us". The banquet passage goes on to children, wife and office; quote
only the dish.

Who said it, safely: "a teacher named Epictetus, who had been born a slave, as recorded by his
student Arrian". He wrote nothing himself. Not safe: "Epictetus wrote".

Traps: "It's not what happens to you, but how you react to it that matters" is a modern
paraphrase credited to Epictetus; it is not his wording. The nearest real text is Enchiridion 5,
verified on the same page: "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of
things." Its next sentence is about death (Socrates and the terror of death), so quote only the
first sentence. "Wealth consists not in having great possessions" is a popular misquotation.

Respect: no living-scripture concern. His enslavement is a fact of his life; it is identity and
hardship material and should not be played for anything.

Saying it aloud: ep-ik-TEE-tus; en-KYE-rih-dee-on. Can be told as "a Greek teacher".

How sure: high.

## epicurus · Greek (Epicurean) · Epicurus · 341–270 BCE; translations 1925 and 1917

serves: the table

The idea: Of all the things wisdom provides for a happy life, the most important by far is
friendship. In a saying preserved by Seneca, Epicurus says to think harder about whom you eat with
than about what you eat, because eating alone is the life of a lion or a wolf.

Source: Principal Doctrines 27, preserved in Diogenes Laertius, Lives of the Eminent Philosophers,
book 10; Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius, letter 19, section 10.

Quotation: "Of all the means which are procured by wisdom to ensure happiness throughout the whole of life, by far the most important is the acquisition of friends."
  R. D. Hicks, Diogenes Laërtius: Lives of Eminent Philosophers (Loeb), 1925; public domain in the US
  checked at: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_X
  Also verified: "You must reflect carefully beforehand with whom you are to eat and drink,
  rather than what you are to eat and drink. For a dinner of meats without the company of a
  friend is like the life of a lion or a wolf." Richard M. Gummere, Seneca's Epistles (Loeb),
  1917; public domain; at https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_19

Other translations: Some translate Doctrine 27 as "the possession of friendship" rather than "the
acquisition of friends"; the sense is the same.

Who said it, safely: "the Greek philosopher Epicurus" for Doctrine 27 (a list of his teachings,
passed down in a later biography); "the Roman writer Seneca quotes Epicurus as saying" for the
dinner line. Epicurus is historical; his own books are almost all lost.

Traps: "Epicurean" in modern English means a lover of fine food; Epicurus taught simple living
(the same Wikisource page says he was content with bread and water and asked for "a little pot of
cheese" as a treat). "It is not so much our friends' help that helps us as the confidence of
their help" is a real Vatican Saying (34), but it was not checked here. "Death is nothing to us"
is real, and out of bounds.

Respect: no living-scripture concern.

Saying it aloud: ep-ih-KYOOR-us; SEN-eh-kuh. The dinner line can be told without the name.

How sure: high.

## heraclitus · Greek (pre-Socratic) · Heraclitus of Ephesus · c. 500 BCE; translation 1920

serves: uncertainty

The idea: Everything flows: you cannot step twice into the same river, because new water is always
flowing in. He also says things rest by changing, and that even a barley drink separates if it is
not stirred.

Source: fragments as numbered by Bywater and Burnet: 41–42 (the river), 83 (rests by changing),
84 (the posset). In the Diels–Kranz numbering scholars now use, the river is B12 and B91, the
rest B84a and B125.

Quotation: "You cannot step twice into the same rivers; for fresh waters are ever flowing in upon you."
  John Burnet, Early Greek Philosophy, 3rd edition 1920; public domain
  checked at: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Early_Greek_Philosophy/Herakleitos_of_Ephesos
  Also verified, same page: "It rests by changing." (83) / "Even the posset separates if it is
  not stirred." (84)

Other translations: Some scholars think only B12 is Heraclitus's own wording ("Upon those who step
into the same rivers, different and again different waters flow") and the "cannot step twice"
form is Plato's later summary. Burnet's fragment 41–42 combines them.

Who said it, safely: "the Greek thinker Heraclitus" or "a fragment of Heraclitus, preserved by later
writers". He is historical; only fragments quoted by others survive.

Traps: "No man ever steps in the same river twice, for it's not the same river and he's not the
same man" is a modern expansion, not a fragment. "The only constant in life is change" is not
Heraclitus's wording. "Panta rhei" ("everything flows") is a later summary of his view, not a
surviving fragment. Fragment 58 on the same page is about doctors charging fees for cutting and
burning the sick; out of bounds.

Respect: no living-scripture concern.

Saying it aloud: hair-uh-KLY-tus. The river can be told without the name.

How sure: high for wording; medium for which form is his own (a live scholarly question).

## hesiod · Greek (archaic poetry) · Hesiod, Works and Days · c. 700 BCE; translation 1914

serves: keep making

The idea: A farmer-poet tells his brother to work, because work brings plenty and idleness brings
hunger, and work is no disgrace. The road to goodness is long, steep and rough at first, and easier
once you reach the top.

Source: Works and Days, line 311 (work is no disgrace); lines 287–292 (the steep path).

Quotation: "Work is no disgrace: it is idleness which is a disgrace."
  Hugh G. Evelyn-White, Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns and Homerica (Loeb), 1914; public domain
  checked at: https://gutenberg.pglaf.org/3/4/348/348-0.txt (Project Gutenberg ebook 348; the e-text puts a stray footnote number, "1308", just before this sentence)
  Also verified, same page: "long and steep is the path that leads to her, and it is rough at the
  first; but when a man has reached the top, then is she easy to reach, though before that she was
  hard."

Other translations: The Greek ἔργον means farm work and toil; it is not "craft" or "art".

Who said it, safely: "the Greek poet Hesiod, in a poem called Works and Days". He is usually
taken as a real person, though little about him is certain beyond what the poem says.

Traps: The poem is addressed to his brother Perses after a quarrel over their inheritance and
complains of "bribe-swallowing" lords who judge unjustly. That is the poem's frame and it is about
justice and a family dispute; out of bounds. "Work" here is also about avoiding hunger and getting
rich, which touches jobs and money.

Respect: no living-scripture concern.

Saying it aloud: HEE-see-od. Can be told as "an old Greek farmer-poet".

How sure: high.

## After the cards

### Pairs and contrasts

- The river and its changing water appears three times, independently: Heraclitus (Greece, c. 500
  BCE), the Analects 9.16 (China, the Master by a stream) and the Hōjōki opening (Japan, 1212).
- Tarfon (do your part of a work you will not finish) and Tirukkural 612 (do not leave a work
  half done) pull in different directions: Tarfon releases you from finishing, the Kural warns
  against abandoning.
- The Gita (act, but not for the fruit) and Epictetus (some things are not in our power) give
  nearly the same advice from different premises: one is duty to a god, the other is a theory of
  what belongs to us.
- The Huainanzi horse and Dao De Jing 58 say fortune and misfortune turn into each other;
  Ecclesiastes 11:6 says you cannot know which effort will prosper. Both counsel acting without
  knowing, but the Chinese texts lean toward calm and Ecclesiastes toward sowing anyway.
- Epicurus (eat with friends, not alone), Tirukkural chapter 9 (eating alone with guests outside
  is abhorred), Hávamál 47 (man is man's delight) and Ecclesiastes 9:7 (eat thy bread with joy)
  all put the shared meal or companion near the centre of a good life. The Hōjōki is the
  dissent: its author leaves people and the city for a hut.
- The Gilgamesh ale-wife's speech and Ecclesiastes 9:7–9 are so close that scholars, including
  Jastrow on the checked page, have long compared them.

### Left out

- **Nahua thought on "the slippery earth" (Florentine Codex, book 6; León-Portilla).** The only
  full English translation (Anderson and Dibble, 1969) and León-Portilla's renderings are in
  copyright, and no page showing the passage could be opened. Not sourced properly, so left out.
- **Māori whakataukī "He aha te mea nui o te ao? He tangata, he tangata, he tangata."** An oral
  proverb, widely credited to Meri Ngāroto of Te Aupōuri; no published source page showing it
  could be opened, and the attribution and fuller form are discussed by her descendants. Also
  said to have been spoken in a time of war. Left out rather than given secondhand.
- **Ubuntu, "a person is a person through other people" (umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu).** A real
  Nguni proverb, but its written statements are modern (20th century, Tutu and others), and "I am
  because we are" is a modern English slogan (often traced to John Mbiti, 1969, who wrote "I am,
  because we are; and since we are, therefore I am"); not checked at a page. Left out.
- **Aristotle on friendship (Nicomachean Ethics 8–9).** Real and relevant, but the shelf already
  has five Greek and Roman cards, and "a friend is a single soul dwelling in two bodies" is
  Diogenes Laertius's report, not the Ethics.
- **Mencius, the farmer of Song who pulled up his sprouts to help them grow (2A2).** A good
  parable of patience; no public-domain translation page opened (ctext.org was behind a bot
  check), so left out.
- **Rumi.** Most English "Rumi" in circulation is loose modern versioning (Coleman Barks and
  others) or invented; not suitable.
- **Doubtful or fake lines met on the way** (none usable): "A journey of a thousand miles
  begins with a single step" in that wording (a smoothing of Dao De Jing 64, see the card); "It
  does not matter how slowly you go as long as you do not stop" (not Confucius); "Holding on to
  anger is like grasping a hot coal" (not the Buddha); "No man ever steps in the same river
  twice, for it's not the same river and he's not the same man" (not Heraclitus); "Do not be
  daunted by the enormity of the world's grief…" (modern paraphrase, not the Talmud); "Everything
  we hear is an opinion, not a fact" (not Marcus Aurelius); the "Maybe" farmer (modern retelling
  of the Huainanzi); "Eat, drink and be merry, for tomorrow we die" (not one verse anywhere);
  "Nature does not hurry, yet everything is accomplished" (not Laozi).

- Which of Pope, Drew and Lazarus wrote the prose lines under the Tirukkural couplets; the online
  editions credit them jointly.
- Diels–Kranz numbers for the Heraclitus fragments, and the claim that only B12 is his own
  wording, are from general knowledge, not from a page fetched for this file.
- Vatican Saying 34 of Epicurus, the Mbiti wording under Ubuntu, and every "modern paraphrase"
  origin named in the traps (Shapiro, Watts, Goldsmith, Hays) are from general knowledge and were
  not checked at a page. They are named only so a writer knows not to use the lines.
- Dates of composition for the Gita, Ecclesiastes, the Dao De Jing, the Dhammapada and the
  Tirukkural are scholarly ranges, not checked for this file.
- Background facts given under "Who said it, safely" (for example Chōmei's Pure Land Buddhism,
  Epictetus's enslavement, Arrian as recorder, the Codex Regius date, Liu An's court) are standard
  reference facts from general knowledge, not checked at a page for this file. The Zhuangzi
  "flow with whatever may happen" trap is likewise from general knowledge.
- Pronunciations are best effort for a speech engine, not checked against a dictionary.
