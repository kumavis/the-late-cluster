# 6. Joke craft

Illustrations in this chapter are constructed and set in an invented allotment society. They show a
principle. They are not material and not templates: do not reuse their shape, their subject or
their wording.

## The basic shape: fact, turn, punch, picture

1. **Fact.** A true thing from the window, said quickly and plainly.
2. **Turn.** A second fact or a reframing that points somewhere unexpected.
3. **Punch.** Short, slower, strongest word last.
4. **Picture.** Something lands on screen on the punch word.

> *Illustration.* "The allotment society voted to ban gnomes." (fact) / "The vote was held on plot
> nine." (turn; plot nine appears, empty) / "Plot nine has forty gnomes." (punch; forty gnomes
> appear on the word "forty", and a label: QUORUM · 41)

Write all four. If you cannot say what the picture is, the joke is not finished.

## What the voice does and what the screen does

The synthesized voice is slow, a little smeared, and loses about one word in five
([voices](07-voices.md)). Pictures are crisp. So:

- **The voice says the setup and the punch,** in short common words.
- **The screen shows the evidence and carries glance-sized extras:** a number, a label, a three-word
  verdict. These can be jokes the voice does not say.
- **The screen is not for reading.** Anything longer than a glance is spoken or it is not there
  ([television](08-direction.md#television)). A second joke that needs a sentence belongs in
  somebody's mouth.

## Specificity

Real beats invented. Small beats big. Named beats generic.

| generic | specific |
|---|---|
| "Everyone's growing courgettes." | "The seed swap listed a hundred and twelve packets. A hundred and nine were courgette." |
| "The committee loves a rule." | "There are three rules about wheelbarrows. Two of them are about the same wheelbarrow." |

Take the specifics from the content card. Never invent a number about a real person or project. If
the card does not have it, write a joke that does not need it.

## Escalation

Establish a pattern, break it, then break the break. Change **one variable per step**: timing, then
size, then scope.

> *Illustration.* The marrow weigh-in. The first marrow weighs four kilos. The second weighs nine.
> The third arrives in a wheelbarrow. The fourth is the wheelbarrow, painted green.

Three steps is usual. Five is the most a segment can hold.

## Characters make jokes

Give the punch to whoever the fact bothers or delights most. The same fact is a different joke from
Sibyl, who cares that procedure was not followed, from Eppy, who wants to know the record, and from
Larry, who just wanted to get through his question.

A real person's own line is a punch only when the show's setup recreates the occasion it was said on
(R4). Otherwise the house characters take the punches around it.

## Demonstrate, don't describe

Do not tell the viewer that something is funny, odd or delightful. Show it happening, preferably
twice, the second time to someone it should not happen to.

> *Illustration.* Don't have Larry say the society's noticeboard is passive-aggressive. Have Sibyl
> read three notices aloud in order, each one replying to the one before, while the camera moves
> down the board.

The same goes for things people made: the joke is in watching the thing work.

## Buttons

Every segment ends on one short line, six words or fewer, deadpan, that tags the segment's main idea
or calls back a number from earlier in the same segment.

## Callbacks

A callback pays off something planted **earlier in the same episode**, ideally by a different
character. A fact stated in one segment and returned to in another ties an episode together cheaply.
Nothing is ever called back from an earlier episode
([do not repeat the show](03-episode-shape.md#do-not-repeat-the-show)).

## Anti-patterns

**Reference without a joke.** Naming a thing and waiting for recognition.

**Explaining the meme.** If it needs three lines of explanation, it is a lecture. Choose other
material, or let a picture explain while the voice does the joke.

**The post as the whole joke.** A house character reads out a funny post and reacts. Reading a post
aloud is right; stopping there is not. Put something of the show's around it: a setup that recreates
its occasion, a topper, a demonstration, a frame that takes it more seriously than it asked.

**The audience as the punchline.** Jokes about the viewers' habits in general. Use one specific
thing that happened.

**Dunking.** On a person, a rival platform, a lab, a bad take. Off-tone, and it ages badly.

**Generic AI jokes.** The cluster has heard them all. The show's jokes about machines are about one
particular machine doing one particular thing this week.

**The show as its own subject.** Jokes about the show being a show, about its makers, about its
medium, about its own rules. They are the easiest jokes to reach for and the likeliest to have been
made already. At most one in an episode, and only when this window's material leads there.

**Signatures.** A line written because a character "always says it", a bit placed because the show
"always does it". You cannot know that, and if you are right it is a repeat.

**The summing-up line.** A character telling the viewer what the episode or the segment was about.

**Engine-dependent punchlines.** A punch on a word the engine says badly ([voices](07-voices.md)).

**Long setups.** More than two lines before the turn. Cut the first; it is usually context a picture
can carry.

## A checklist for each joke

- [ ] Is the fact true, and on a content card?
- [ ] Is there a turn, and can I name it in five words?
- [ ] Is the punch the last word or two, and short?
- [ ] What lands on screen, and on which word?
- [ ] Is everything on screen either spoken or a glance?
- [ ] Would a viewer who missed the week get it from the line and the picture?
- [ ] Would the person in it repost it?
- [ ] Can the engine say the punch word?
- [ ] Did this come from the material, or from a feeling about what the show does?
