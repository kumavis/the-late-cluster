# 3. The shape of an episode

Everything here is subject to [the first rule](00-constitution.md#1-only-include-it-if-its-interesting):
only include it if it's interesting.

## The frame

Four things carry from one episode to the next. They are kept for now, by the owner's choice.

1. **The name:** *The Late Cluster with Larry Nx*.
2. **The house cast:** Larry Nx, Sibyl Ant, Hy Oid & The Formants and Eppy Glottis
   ([the cast](02-cast.md)).
3. **The studio.**
4. **Its vector look:** light on black, drawn in lines ([direction](08-direction.md#the-vector-look)).

That is the whole frame. The labels, disclaimer and credits required by the constitution (Article 4)
are not frame; they are law, and they are in every episode.

Nothing else is standing structure. There is no fixed way to open or close, no fixed running order,
no line that is said every week, no fixture on screen all night, no ritual. If something feels like
it must be there because this show always has it, it is not in the frame, and the rule
[do not repeat the show](#do-not-repeat-the-show) applies to it.

## The standing segments

Three segments are drafted every week. They are formats, written out in full in
[formats](04-formats.md#the-three-standing-segments), and each is new inside every time.

- **The interview:** one community member, constructed for now from their public posts.
- **Top Chicken:** the game's results in the window, with the game around them.
- **Music:** a song in a genre the show has not just used, on a theme from the window.

They are standing, not guaranteed. A standing segment that is not interesting this week is cut, and
the scorecard records why.

The rest of an episode is chosen from the window's best material, in whatever shape carries it: one
from [the shelf](04-formats.md#the-shelf), or a new one.

## Length

Aim for **about eight minutes**. The ceiling is **ten**. There is no minimum: an episode is as long
as its interesting material, and padding fails. The first rule decides.

Planning figures, to be corrected by the renderer:

| piece | usual length |
|---|---|
| the open | 10 to 30 s |
| the interview | 70 to 110 s |
| Top Chicken | 60 to 90 s |
| music | 50 to 90 s |
| each other segment (usually two or three) | 45 to 90 s |
| the close, before credits | 20 to 40 s |

- Speech runs at about 2.5 to 2.8 words a second while someone is talking.
- With reactions, music and staging, a segment carries about **1.6 to 2.2 spoken words per second**
  of running time. A 70-second segment is 110 to 150 words; eight minutes is roughly 770 to 1,050.
- Music is expensive: a song carries few spoken words for its length.
- Write to the figure, then render. The renderer's timeline is the truth.

## Building the rundown

More segments are drafted than an episode needs, and reviewers select. One writer then assembles the
selected drafts: the order, the open, the close and the joins.

**The order** is decided each week from what was selected. Nothing has a home position.

- Adjacent segments differ in texture: who presents, how loud, how fast, what is on screen. Never two
  desk-and-graphics segments in the same register back to back.
- The biggest laugh belongs in the back half.
- A quiet moment needs louder neighbours.
- Place the music by its energy. A loud song can be the peak near the end. A quiet one sits better
  in the middle, away from a quiet close.

**The open** has two jobs: within about fifteen seconds a new viewer knows what they are watching
(the title, the host), and the first piece of this window's material has begun. How it does them is
a fresh decision every week.

**The close** has two jobs: end on the last strong thing, and run the credits the constitution
requires. It does not review the episode. How it ends is a fresh decision every week.

**Joins.** Segments cut straight into one another, so:

- every segment ends on a button: one short line, then a sting or a reaction;
- a segment that changes presenter hands off by name;
- the first line of a segment tells a new viewer what it is.

**A runner inside one episode** is allowed when the window supplies one: something that can be said
or shown in five words, planted early, raised once or twice, and paid off. Three appearances make a
runner; two make a callback. It belongs to that episode alone.

## Do not repeat the show

**A joke, runner, catchphrase, staging device, segment shape or guest from the previous episode is
not used again, and the show does not lean on its own history.** No callbacks to earlier episodes,
no "as always", no bit that works only if you saw last week. The check is strictest about the
previous episode and does not stop there: a joke the show has done at any time is a repeat.

*Why.* The audience saw last week. A joke that comes back tells them the show has one idea, and a
show that keeps its favourite devices turns week by week into a remake of its first episode. The
subject of the show is the community's week. Every second spent on the show's own traditions is
taken from that.

### What this means for a writer

You have not seen what the show has done, and you will not: writers do not read earlier episodes or
the record of them. Reviewers do. The rule is therefore kept in two halves, and yours is this.

1. **Write from this window's material and from the characters.** Every premise should trace to a
   content card, or to who a house character is. "This is the kind of thing the show does" is not a
   source.
2. **Do not reach for anything that feels like a signature.** Late-night shows are full of them: a
   sign-off line, a nightly list, a desk toy, a sidekick's stock reply, a ritual way to start. If
   something you are writing feels like one of those for this show, a thing a character "always"
   says or a bit you assume is expected, that feeling of "this is how the show goes" is the
   warning. You cannot know how the show goes. What you are feeling is the genre's default or your
   own guess, and both are exactly where a repeat is most likely.
3. **Do not invent a past.** No line refers to an earlier episode, a tradition, "last week", "once
   again" or "as usual", and nothing on screen points back at one.
4. **Do not try to find out.** Do not read earlier episodes, their scripts, their builds or the
   ledger, and do not ask a reviewer what was done.
5. **Expect to be sent back.** Two reviewers with the full record give every shortlisted idea, and
   later every selected script, a verdict: *fresh*, *turned* or *repeat*. A repeat is blocked. It
   comes back with a line or two saying what kind of thing it repeats, and no more than you need to
   steer clear. This is not a mark against the writing. It is the only way you could have known.
6. **Replace, don't disguise.** When something is blocked, go back to the material and write
   something else. The same joke with different nouns is the same joke.

A standing segment recurs as a format, not as a script. Its subject, its structure, its staging and
its jokes are new every week; build each from that week's material and not from an idea of how the
segment usually goes.

### The one exception: a joke turned on its head

Something the show has done may come back once if it is **turned**: it inverts the expectation the
earlier joke set up, or it makes fun of the earlier joke itself. Bringing the joke back with a wink,
smaller, or in a new costume is not a turn. An episode carries at most one turned item.

A writer cannot turn what they have not seen, so a turn only ever starts with a reviewer.

1. A reviewer blocks one of your items as a repeat. If the episode's one turn is still unused and
   the reviewer thinks the earlier thing can bear it, the note says a turn is open, and describes the
   earlier thing just enough: what it set up, and what a viewer now expects when it starts.
2. You may then answer in one of two ways: replace the item, which is always acceptable, or propose
   a turn. A proposal states the expectation in a sentence and says how your version defeats it or
   makes the old joke the target.
3. The reviewers judge the proposal. It must be a complete joke for someone who never saw the
   original, and it must be one moment, not a thread through the episode.

Never write a "subversion" of something you only suppose the show does. Without a reviewer's
description you are either inventing the show's past or walking into a repeat.

*Illustration, constructed, set in an invented allotment society and not to be reused.* Suppose a
reviewer's note said: "Blocked, repeat, runner. Earlier, whenever anyone said the word 'frost', a
pane fell out of the greenhouse. A turn is open." A turn: someone says "frost", everyone flinches
and looks at the greenhouse, and nothing happens, because there are no panes left; the secretary
reads out the glazing bill. Not a turn: the pane falls out on the word "ice" instead.

## Variety in people

**The show is about a community, not about its own regulars.** Fresh people are preferred, and the
same few people should not be in too often.

Nobody is barred for having appeared before. Shutting past guests out would be too strong: someone
who made the best thing of the window belongs in the episode whoever they are. What the show avoids
is leaning on the same handful of accounts. Two fixed points follow:

- someone whose puppet spoke in the previous episode does not speak in this one (their post may
  still be shown);
- most of the people featured in an episode, in puppets or on cards, are new to the show.

*Why.* A whole community should not look, on the show, like six people. Viewers who see
the same accounts every week learn that the show has an in-crowd, which is the opposite of what it
believes about membership. And the best-known accounts are the easiest to find material from, so
without a rule the show drifts to them.

*In practice.* The research marks every author with three facts: `featuredPrevious` (whether they
spoke or were shown in the previous episode), `featuredEarlier` (the same for any episode before
that) and `timesFeatured` (how many times in all). **Prefer people those marks show as new.** The
marks say only that someone was on. What anyone said or did on the show you do not know, and do not
need.

- Choose people for what they did in this window, not for how well known they are.
- When two pieces of material are equally strong, take the one whose people are unmarked or least
  marked. Read the "new and smaller voices" cards as carefully as the busiest threads. (A small
  account appears only as the hero of a moment: R7.)
- Someone marked as having spoken in the previous episode is not cast to speak in this one.
- Spread the attention inside the episode too. If one account is in three segments, the episode is
  about them.
- Reviewers apply the same record at the gate. They may tell you that someone is not available to
  speak, or that someone has been on a lot lately and another choice would be better. It is no
  judgement on the person.
- The winner of Top Chicken is the game's choice, not ours, and is always named.

## Evolving the show

The constitution (Article 6) says that every episode looks back at the last one, tests one idea, and
may amend this DNA. This is how that works when writers do not see the show's history.

### Who looks back

The look back is done by reviewers and the project manager. They can read the earlier episodes,
their scorecards and what the community said. What reaches a writer from it is deliberately small
and points forward: the episode's experiment, or a thing to avoid. It never says what went down well.
"People liked that, do more" is how a show starts repeating itself.

### One experiment per episode

Every episode tests one idea.

- **A reviewer proposes it,** once the shortlist exists, and writes it to
  `production/experiment.md`: what to try, why, and a measure someone else can check. The reviewer
  can see what the show has tried; the file describes no earlier episode, so writers can read it.
- **Writers receive it in their briefs and build it in.** Where it touches your segment, run it as
  written. Do not swap it for an experiment of your own, and do not soften it to be safe.
- **The final reviewer judges it** in row B12 of the [scorecard](10-process.md#the-scorecard): was it
  run on screen, and does its measure hold?

A good experiment is:

- **Stated against this bible, not against earlier episodes.** It reads: "the bible says X; this
  episode tries Y instead, in this one place." A new segment shape is the most natural one there is.
- **One variable.** Change one thing and hold the rest, or nobody can tell what worked.
- **Sized to an episode.** One segment, one element, one habit; reversible next week.
- **Measured by something someone else can check.** "A cold viewer can say in one sentence what the
  segment showed" is a measure. "It's funnier" is not. Whoever proposed it does not judge it.
- **Never constitutional.** An experiment cannot relax any article of the constitution or any rule in
  chapter 9, including the show's own review.

"Didn't work" and "can't tell" are results. Reviewers record the verdict, with the evidence, in
their own record after the episode has aired.

### When a result changes the DNA

- Change the bible when an experiment worked and would work in another week, or when it showed that
  a rule here is wrong. Edit the chapter where the rule lives, in the episode's pull request. Keep
  the edit small: a sentence, a row, a paragraph.
- **Write the change as craft.** State the principle. If it needs an example, construct one. An
  amendment never describes what an episode did, quotes it, or names who was in it: that evidence
  belongs to the reviewers. An amendment that would let a writer reproduce the episode it came from
  is refused.
- To retire something, delete it from the bible. Git keeps the original.
- The house cast and the studio are kept by the owner's choice. An episode may propose a change to
  them; the owner decides.

### Two ways to fail

- **Drift:** becoming a different show by accident. Signs: changes with no stated reason; several
  changes at once; the [one-page version](README.md#the-show-on-one-page) no longer describing what
  is being made.
- **Hardening:** becoming a formula. Signs a writer can see from here: reaching for signatures;
  experiments that are cosmetic; every segment taken from the shelf; a bible amendment that reads
  like a description of an episode.

### How little this bible knows

Most of the numbers and habits in these chapters rest on very little evidence. They are starting
points. Challenging one is a good experiment.

### Carrying things forward

Writers carry nothing forward. A builder may reuse staging code only when the episode's plan names
the file and gives a reason. A prop, shot or helper lives in the episode that made it and moves into
the shared studio only when a second episode needs it.
